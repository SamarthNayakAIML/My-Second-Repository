The primary difference between Pelton, Francis, and Kaplan turbines lies in the water head (elevation drop) and flow rate they are designed to handle, which determines whether they operate by impulse or reaction. How Each Turbine Works & When to Use It

• Pelton Turbine (High Head, Low Flow): Water is forced through nozzles to create high-velocity jets that strike spoon-shaped buckets on the wheel in open air. It converts pure kinetic energy into rotation. Best suited for steep, mountainous terrain with tall waterfalls or long penstocks. • Francis Turbine (Medium Head, Medium Flow): Water enters a spiral casing radially through guide vanes, passes over curved runner blades, and exits axially through a draft tube. It uses both pressure and kinetic energy. Because it covers the widest range of heads, it is the most commonly installed turbine globally. Key Engineering Trade-offs

• Part-Load Efficiency: Kaplan and Pelton turbines maintain high efficiency across varying flow rates (Kaplan via adjustable blades; Pelton by shutting off individual nozzles). Francis turbines suffer efficiency drops and severe vibration if run outside their optimal flow range. [1] (/goto?url=CAESmAEBxCJF1-M3FPGZATI3qvXuO2ULAs2FfZajVccYZxs_fQDdSjKDI-5DOQCbwM_DVDr6hT6SJm3W8yZLAWZOCmJst-nHzZv0bfCjz6D_JxUnRa4BUYxMN9PxAFskSlDvslW6aSWO1pkKQaZxe2vnkaF52_iB3CB-DGB1Avp3HUUQ4eTxeZt1W6kz4Ys_wmeGS-BsFcRhVnY31g)
• Cavitation Risk: Because Francis and Kaplan turbines operate completely submerged under changing pressures, they are prone to cavitation (vapor bubbles damaging metal blades) and require a draft tube to recover exit pressure. Pelton runners spin in atmospheric air, eliminating reaction cavitation.
